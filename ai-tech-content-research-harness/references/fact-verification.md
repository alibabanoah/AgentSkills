# Fact Verification

Use this reference before producing content based on strong claims, public figures, research data, company facts, viral cases, or controversy.

## Claim labels

Label important claims:

- `confirmed`: supported by primary or authoritative source.
- `reported`: credible media reports it, but primary evidence is unavailable.
- `interpreted`: a reasonable analysis derived from facts.
- `speculative`: plausible but not verified.
- `unsupported`: do not use as fact.

## Source type matrix

Use this matrix when deciding how strongly a claim can be stated:

- Primary source: official blog, paper, filing, transcript, model card, GitHub repo, product page, conference talk, original post. Can support factual claims within its scope.
- Institutional source: university, regulator, public agency, standards body, labor statistics, reputable research institute. Can support data and policy claims, but preserve methodology limits.
- Reputable reporting: named journalist, established outlet, clear sourcing. Can support "reported" claims, but verify with primary sources when the claim is central.
- Expert interpretation: analyst, researcher, VC, newsletter, podcast. Can support analysis or viewpoint, not raw fact unless sourced.
- Social signal: viral post, comment, short clip, community discussion. Use for trend discovery and audience sentiment, not proof.
- Unknown/anonymous source: do not use except to say the claim is unverified.

## Verification workflow

1. Identify every claim that could be challenged: numbers, dates, quotes, company actions, research findings, job-loss figures, funding, product capability, public reaction.
2. Trace each claim to the earliest reliable source.
3. Check whether later summaries changed wording, scope, or certainty.
4. Preserve the exact scope: sample, region, time period, industry, company, model version.
5. Separate fact from interpretation in the final content.
6. Keep a "risk note" for claims that should be softened or omitted.

## Minimum verification standards

- For a quote: find the original post, video, transcript, or reputable full-context report.
- For a statistic: identify source, sample, geography, time period, methodology, and whether it is an estimate.
- For a company/product capability: verify from official demo, docs, release notes, technical report, or independent test.
- For a funding or valuation claim: verify from company announcement, filing, reputable business reporting, or funding database.
- For employment impact: prefer labor-data providers, academic studies, official statistics, or named company reports; avoid extrapolating one company to the whole economy.
- For creator success stories: verify original work, creator account, date, budget/timeline if claimed, third-party recognition, and whether commercial deals are confirmed.

## High-risk claims

Require stronger evidence for:

- "X said Y" quotes from famous people
- "jobs disappeared by N%"
- "company shut down school/product/project because of AI"
- "AI is 1000x cheaper"
- "a creator got a Hollywood deal"
- "a technology can restore sight, revive people, or replace doctors/teachers"
- accusations of fraud, exploitation, negligence, illegal conduct, or deliberate harm

## Safe wording patterns

Use:

- "according to..."
- "the company says..."
- "the report estimates..."
- "one interpretation is..."
- "the stronger signal is..."
- "this does not prove X, but it suggests Y..."

Avoid:

- fake certainty
- invented causality
- exact numbers without source
- clipped quotes without context
- making a person or company look guilty without verified evidence

## Exaggeration boundary

Allowed attention techniques:

- sharper contrast
- vivid analogy
- stronger stakes
- narrative tension
- provocative question
- "this may be the beginning of..."

Not allowed:

- changing facts
- inflating numbers
- inventing quotes
- presenting predictions as facts
- turning weak correlation into direct cause
- hiding uncertainty that matters
