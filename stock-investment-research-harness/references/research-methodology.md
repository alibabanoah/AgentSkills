# Research Methodology

Use this reference to explain or build the method behind new-stock research conclusions.

## Method foundation

Base the method on four public-research principles:

1. Research must be timely, objective, well sourced, internally consistent, and clear about assumptions, facts versus opinions, risks, and conflicts.
2. Company analysis should start from primary filings and financial statements, including MD&A, risk factors, audited financials, market-risk disclosures, footnotes, auditor language, and material-event filings.
3. Valuation should connect narrative to numbers. The analyst must identify cash flows, growth, risk, reinvestment, and terminal assumptions rather than relying on a price chart alone.
4. Relative valuation can be useful, but comparable-company multiples need controls for differences in growth, profitability, risk, capital intensity, accounting, and business mix.

## Thesis-generation chain

Produce every new-stock conclusion through this chain:

```text
1. Mandate
2. Universe
3. Source evidence
4. Business diagnosis
5. Value-driver hypothesis
6. Valuation test
7. Catalyst and timing test
8. Risk and falsification test
9. Ranking and conclusion tier
10. Monitoring plan
```

### 1. Mandate

Define the investor problem before selecting stocks:

- geography and listing venue
- time horizon
- risk tolerance
- liquidity and market-cap range
- sector exclusions
- desired role: growth, value, income, defensive, cyclical, special situation, hedge

If the mandate is missing, use a balanced long-only public-equity default and state that assumption.

### 2. Universe

Build a broad candidate list from multiple independent routes:

- sector or macro theme
- quality and return-on-capital screens
- earnings or margin inflection
- valuation reset
- balance-sheet improvement
- capital return
- regulatory or product catalyst
- defensive cash-flow stability

Avoid single-source idea generation. A stock should not pass only because it is popular, trending, or recently up.

### 3. Source evidence

Gather current evidence from:

- company filings and investor materials
- earnings releases and transcripts
- financial statements and footnotes
- regulator or exchange disclosures
- industry data and credible market data
- high-quality news only for recent event context

Separate reported facts, management claims, consensus expectations, market prices, and analyst interpretation.

### 4. Business diagnosis

Answer:

- How does the company make money?
- Who are the customers?
- What drives demand?
- What are the unit economics?
- Does the business have pricing power, scale, network effects, switching costs, regulation, brand, cost advantage, or no durable moat?
- Is growth organic, cyclical, acquisition-driven, price-driven, or accounting-driven?
- How capital-intensive is the business?

The goal is not description. The goal is to identify which driver can change intrinsic value or market perception.

### 5. Value-driver hypothesis

Convert the business diagnosis into one explicit hypothesis:

```text
This stock is worth researching because the market may be mispricing [driver], and the key evidence is [evidence].
```

Common drivers:

- revenue growth durability
- margin expansion
- operating leverage
- return on invested capital
- free cash flow conversion
- lower risk or lower discount rate
- balance-sheet repair
- reinvestment runway
- capital return
- sum-of-the-parts gap
- earnings quality improvement

Reject vague theses such as "AI leader", "cheap stock", "good company", or "price has fallen a lot" unless they are translated into a measurable driver.

### 6. Valuation test

Use valuation to test the hypothesis, not to decorate it.

Choose methods by company type:

- stable cash-flow company: DCF, dividend model, free-cash-flow yield, peer multiples
- high-growth company: scenario DCF, unit economics, revenue-to-margin bridge, dilution analysis
- cyclical company: mid-cycle earnings, replacement cost, normalized margins, balance-sheet stress
- financial company: book value, ROE, credit quality, net interest margin, capital ratios
- conglomerate or multi-segment company: sum-of-the-parts
- distressed company: liquidity, debt maturity, recovery value, dilution and refinancing scenarios

Always include base, upside, and downside cases. Identify the few assumptions that drive most of the value.

### 7. Catalyst and timing test

Identify why the market might close the gap:

- earnings inflection
- guidance revision
- product cycle
- regulatory approval or resolution
- margin recovery
- capital return
- restructuring
- debt refinancing
- sector cycle turn
- index inclusion or liquidity improvement

If there is no plausible catalyst, classify as long-term watchlist unless the valuation margin of safety is unusually large.

### 8. Risk and falsification test

List the conditions that would make the thesis wrong:

- growth slows below threshold
- margins fail to expand
- free cash flow does not convert
- debt or refinancing risk increases
- regulation changes the economics
- customer concentration worsens
- competition compresses returns
- valuation multiple requires unrealistic terminal assumptions
- accounting quality deteriorates

Make the falsification test observable. Avoid "risk: stock may go down" because that is not a thesis test.

### 9. Ranking and conclusion tier

Rank with explicit weights or a qualitative score:

- evidence quality
- business quality
- valuation asymmetry
- catalyst clarity
- balance-sheet safety
- risk severity
- time horizon fit
- confidence level

Use conclusion tiers:

- `A`: high-priority research candidate
- `B`: watchlist; needs price, evidence, or catalyst
- `C`: interesting but high risk
- `D`: avoid for now

### 10. Monitoring plan

For every candidate, define:

- next earnings or filing date
- key KPI to monitor
- valuation level or scenario threshold
- event trigger
- thesis-falsification trigger
- date for scheduled review

## Audit checklist

Before finalizing a new-stock conclusion, verify:

- The thesis is one sentence and falsifiable.
- The key driver is measurable.
- Primary sources support the core facts.
- Valuation assumptions match the business narrative.
- Relative multiples use appropriate peers.
- Risks are specific to the thesis.
- The conclusion tier follows from evidence, not preference.
- The report states what would change the view.

## Authority anchors

Use these sources as conceptual anchors when browsing or citing:

- CFA Institute equity valuation material for valuation process, report quality, internal consistency, assumptions, risks, and objectivity.
- SEC/Investor.gov 10-K and 10-Q guidance for filings, MD&A, risk factors, market-risk disclosures, audited statements, non-GAAP measures, auditor issues, and red flags.
- FINRA investor education for financial statement use and research-report objectivity/conflict principles.
- Aswath Damodaran valuation materials for intrinsic valuation, DCF inputs, cash flow, growth, risk, relative valuation, and the need to connect narrative to numbers.
